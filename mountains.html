Mountain Ranges

A mountain range on Earth

Mountains represent some of Earth's most dramatic landscapes, formed through the slow but powerful movements of tectonic plates over millions of years. The Himalayas, home to Mount Everest, are still growing about 1 cm per year as the Indian plate continues to collide with the Eurasian plate. These high-altitude environments create unique ecosystems that change dramatically with elevation, similar to how desert landscapes transform with minimal changes in altitude.

At their peaks, mountains create their own weather patterns. The famous banner clouds that form on the Matterhorn or Mount Everest are created by winds forced upward over the peaks. This orographic lift also creates rain shadows, where one side of a mountain range may be lush while the other is arid, as seen in Washington's Olympic Mountains versus its eastern canyon lands.

Life in Thin Air

Mountain ecosystems change with altitude in distinct zones:

  • Base: Typically forested areas, similar to lowland forests
  • Montane Zone: Cooler temperatures support coniferous forests
  • Subalpine Zone: Stunted trees (krummholz) and hardy shrubs
  • Alpine Zone: Above treeline, with meadows of wildflowers in summer
  • Nival Zone: Permanent snow and ice, with only the hardiest lichens and algae

Remarkably, some species like the Himalayan jumping spider live permanently above 22,000 feet, surviving in an environment with less than 40% of the oxygen at sea level. Meanwhile, mountain rivers fed by glacial melt provide freshwater to billions of people downstream.

Geological Wonders

Mountains reveal Earth's geological history through their exposed rock layers. The Canadian Rockies display dramatically folded sedimentary rocks, while the granite domes of Yosemite showcase massive plutons that cooled slowly underground before being exposed by erosion.

Volcanic mountains like those in the Cascade Range or the Andes form differently, built up by successive eruptions. These volcanic peaks often have more conical shapes compared to the rugged peaks of folded mountain ranges.

Mountains also serve as water towers for the planet. The snowpack in mountain ranges like the Alps or Rockies stores winter precipitation, releasing it slowly during warmer months to sustain alpine meadows and provide water for agriculture and cities downstream. Climate change is dramatically affecting these natural reservoirs, with glaciers retreating worldwide at unprecedented rates.
